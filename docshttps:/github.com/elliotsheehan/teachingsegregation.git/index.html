Race and Housing in 1900s Roanoke, VA

Research Question: What factors explain twentieth-century residential segregation in Roanoke, Virginia? How did discriminatory tactics and patterns of segregation change over time?

In recent years, the impacts of housing discrimination, interstate highways, and urban renewal have received increasing attention. Scholars like Robert Weaver, Jane Jacobs, and Herbert Gans were among the earliest to document the litany of tactics used by white politicians, bureaucrats, real estate investors, and neighborhood associations alike to segregate American cities.[1] More recently, scholars like Elizabeth Hinton, Mindy Fullilove, and Douglas Appler have demonstrated how communities have responded to destructive urban projects.[2] This project will use Roanoke as a case study for a US history unit, aligned to the C3 Framework and Virginia Standards of Learning (SOLs). I will organize the unit around three distinct but thematically-related modules emphasizing racially restrictive covenants, redlining and mortgage discrimination, and urban renewal and highway construction. Students will identify the factors that have contributed to residential segregation in Roanoke and how segregationist tactics and patterns of segregation have evolved throughout the twentieth century.

Scholars and public historians have made valuable contributions in the documentation of Roanoke’s urban history. In Root Shock: How Tearing Up City Neighborhoods Hurts America, and What We Can Do About It, social psychiatrist Mindy Fullilove features Roanoke as a central case study in her study of the public health impacts of urban renewal. The Gainsboro History Project outlines the history of Roanoke’s Gainsboro neighborhood through an interactive digital exhibit. The Roanoke Public Library conducted an oral history project of Gainsboro residents, who reflected upon the neighborhood and its destructive redevelopment. However, much of the existing literature centers urban renewal and the construction of I-581, and there are no ready-to-use educational resources on urban history in Roanoke. In adapting original research into a publicly available history unit, I will not only expand the temporal scope that previous projects have taken, but also provide a valuable resource to history educators in Southwest Virginia.

The unit will be modeled after the C3 Framework for Social Studies State Standards. Each lesson will follow C3’s Inquiry Design Model (IDM), which engages students in investigating compelling questions, analyzing sources, and developing evidence-based arguments.[3] Though the SOLs are chronologically structured, history educators who teach thematically use the thematic strands from the National Council for the Social Studies to make their curriculum coherent. I will therefore align the modules to the following strands: Time, Continuity, and Change; People, Places, and Environments; Power, Authority, and Governance; and Individuals, Groups, and Institutions. Grounding the unit in these strands facilitates connections across different units. 

Despite the limitations in the SOLS, the 2023 update of the Virginia SOLs for eleventh grade Virginia and United States History provides an opportunity to cover the fraught history of urban development. The content standards are structured such that each strand (i.e. VUS.10 or VUS.16) contains supporting questions that seek to guide students towards mastery. Forming a
“Unit Essential Question” (UEQ) is furthermore considered a best practice in social studies pedagogy. For the purpose of this project, the UEQ will serve as the main inquiry, for in answering my own research question I will be able to provide students with the appropriate tools and material to come to develop their own arguments. Therefore, I have developed the research question (above) from the synthesis of the following content standards and supporting questions provided by the Virginia Department of Education:

Content Standards

VUS.10.d: The student will apply history and social science skills to analyze how the nation grew and changed from the end of Reconstruction through the early 20th century by explaining the social and cultural impact of industrialization including, but not limited to rapid urbanization.

Supporting Question: How did industrialization affect the lives of Americans?

VUS.12.a: The student will apply history and social science skills to understand key international and domestic events, interests, and philosophies during the 1920s and 1930s byanalyzing the attacks on civil liberties including, but not limited to the reemergence of the Ku Klux Klan, the Chicago riot of 1919, Tulsa Race Massacre and the decimation of Black Wall Street, the institution of redlining, and the resulting racial wealth gaps.

Supporting Questions: How did the reemergence of the Ku Klux Klan impact civil rights in the 1920s? What were the causes and consequences of the Tulsa Race Massacre? How did redlining institutionalize discrimination and affect minority communities?

VUS. 12.g: Examining the Great Migration and its influence on the Harlem Renaissance, prompting new trends in literature, music, and art, and the work of writers including, but not limited to Zora Neale Hurston and Langston Hughes.

Supporting Questions: How did African American communities change and thrive despite racial injustice during the 1920s? What were the causes of the Great Migration and how did this movement impact African American communities? How was African American life and culture illustrated by the Harlem Renaissance? What contributions did Zora Neale Hurston and Langston Hughes make to African American culture and society as a whole? How did resistance to social change lead to increased acts of violence during the 1920s?

VUS.17.a: The student will apply history and social science skills to analyze political and social conditions in the United States during the second half of the 20th century and early 21st century by assessing the development of and changes in domestic policies… including, but not limited to… [the] Federal Highway Act of 1956.

Supporting Questions: How have innovations in… technology… impacted American life in the 21st century? What strategies did various groups use to advance their goals for equal treatment, opportunity, and protections?

VUS.16.a: The student will apply history and social science skills to analyze the causes and effects of the Civil Rights Movement by analyzing the origins of the Civil Rights Movement, the effects of segregation, and efforts to desegregate schools, transportation, and public areas.

Supporting Questions: Who were the key leaders and organizations of the modern Civil Rights Movement? What tactics were used to gain public attention and garner support for civil rights legislation?

To align with the IDM framework that culminates in an evidence-based argument, the summative assessment for the unit will take the form of an “unessay.” Students will synthesize the sources from the unit to make an argument about the factors that have led to residential segregation in Roanoke and how they changed over time. As such, students will be working towards the following skill standards: 

Skill Standards
d. investigating and analyzing evidence from multiple sources to construct arguments and draw conclusions
f. determining cause and effect to analyze connections
i. developing products that reflect an understanding of research and content to make real-life connections

In order for students to master the content and skill standards, I will structure the lesson around the following topics and sources:

  1. The first module will focus on racially restrictive covenants in Roanoke. I will begin by contextualizing the rapid industrialization and population growth of Roanoke. Students will understand how the resulting racial strife resulted in violence and discriminatory the use of racially restrictive covenants to segregate neighborhoods and maintain white property values. Students will learn about an 1893 lynching in Roanoke. They will also understand that that, despite housing discrimination, Roanoke’s Black residents built their own thriving pockets within the city, namely Henry Street in Gainsboro. I will use demographic data and newspaper sources and will locate racially restrictive covenants in the City of Roanoke’s deeds office to enable students to identify motivations and patterns of segregation.
  2. The second module will address redlining and mortgage discrimination during the New Deal through the years following World War II. I will adapt the area descriptions and georeferenced HOLC maps from the Mapping Inequality digital history project. Students will use these sources to further identify the changing nature of residential segregation in Roanoke.
  3. The third module will discuss the Interstate Highway System and urban renewal programs and their impacts to the urban landscape throughout the second half of the twentieth century. I will juxtapose Sanborn maps/satellite imagery of Roanoke with current basemaps and provide excerpts of oral history interviews with Gainsboro residents. I will use demographic data to make maps showing a relationship between infrastructure, segregation, and population.

Though Roanoke will be central to the content, there may be instances where I will have to incorporate events occurring in other cities. For example, students will need to also know about the Greenwood neighborhood in Tulsa and 1919 Chicago race riot. I view this as a strength of this project’s design because students will have a local example from which to connect events occurring in other cities.

To build the source sets for this unit, I will combine exisiting research with original. The lessons will contain material from Root Shock, the Gainsboro History project, and Gainsboro oral histories. I will conduct original research at Virginia Tech Special Collections, Roanoke Public Libraries, and City of Roanoke deeds office. One issue I foresee is reproducing archival material on the web, so I will be proactive about obtaining permissions.

Each module will contain ready-to-use lesson materials and activities and a guide for teachers. To best accommodate students, the modules will employ the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and Differentiated Instruction (DI). I will also use tools learned in my digital history course to create the website, including R code and Quarto website building. The project will be publicly available for teachers and students of eleventh grade Virginia and US History. Other intended audiences include the portfolio committee, the Roanoke community, and future employers.
